Michigan Wine Grapes: Riesling, Chardonnay, Reds & Fruit Wines

November 24, 2026 11 min read By the MV Editorial Team
Home›The Vine›Michigan Wine Grapes Guide

Michigan is cool-climate wine country. The lake keeps summers milder and autumns longer than inland farms farther south, which is why aromatic whites, fresher reds, sparkling wine, and orchard fruit wines thrive here. This guide walks through the grapes and styles Michigan is known for — what they taste like, which regions grow them well, and where to pour a glass on your trip.

New to tasting rooms? Start with our Michigan wine tasting guide. Choosing between the Traverse City peninsulas? Read Old Mission vs. Leelanau. For routes, open the wine trails, the regions overview, or the winery directory.

Why Michigan’s Climate Suits These Grapes

Lake Michigan is the quiet partner in almost every bottle. Along the shoreline and the finger-like peninsulas near Traverse City, the water holds heat into fall and softens deep winter cold. That “lake effect” gives grapes a longer hang time without the scorching summers that build heavy, high-alcohol wines elsewhere.

What that means in the glass:

  • Bright acidity. Whites stay crisp; reds stay food-friendly rather than thick and stewy.
  • Aromatic fruit. Riesling, Gewürztraminer, and Pinot Grigio/Gris show floral and orchard notes clearly.
  • Room for sparkling and late-harvest styles. Cool nights help grapes keep the freshness those styles need.

Northern Michigan’s Old Mission and Leelanau trails sit in that moderated climate. Southwest Michigan’s Lake Michigan Shore trail (including the Fennville area) is a bit warmer and often grows a wider range of reds and hybrids alongside whites. For a full map of AVAs and trails, see Regions.

"Think of Michigan wine as cool-climate first: bright whites, lively reds, serious sparkling, and fruit wines that taste like the orchards and berry farms next door."

Michigan White Wines

White wine is where Michigan often makes its first impression. Ask for a dry-to-off-dry flight and you will quickly taste why visitors search for Michigan Riesling and Michigan white wine before anything else.

Riesling

Michigan Riesling is the state’s calling card. Expect citrus, green apple, peach, and white flower aromas, with a clean finish that can range from bone-dry to gently sweet late-harvest. Ice wine and late-harvest pours show up at estates that specialize in the grape.

Old Mission helped build that reputation. Start with Chateau Grand Traverse, known for Riesling, Late Harvest Riesling, Ice Wine, and Pinot Grigio. Nearby, Mari Vineyards and Black Star Farms — Old Mission also list Riesling among their known wines. On Leelanau, look to Good Harbor Vineyards, Shady Lane Cellars (including Sparkling Riesling), and 45 North. Southwest visitors can taste Riesling at Fenn Valley Vineyards in Fennville.

Chardonnay

Michigan Chardonnay tends to be fresher and more orchard-fruit driven than big, buttery California styles — think apple, pear, citrus, and a light creamy note when oak is used. Some tasting rooms pour both unoaked and barrel-influenced versions side by side.

On Old Mission, Chateau Chantal, Bowers Harbor Vineyards (Langley Chardonnay), Brys Estate (Naked Chardonnay), and Bonobo Winery all list Chardonnay among their known wines. On Leelanau, try Leelanau Cellars (Tall Ship Chardonnay) or Aurora Cellars.

Pinot Grigio / Pinot Gris

Whether the label says Pinot Grigio or Pinot Gris, you are usually looking at a light, food-friendly white — pear, citrus zest, and a soft mineral finish. Michigan Pinot Grigio is an easy first pour for guests who want something dry and approachable.

Find it at Chateau Grand Traverse, Peninsula Cellars, and Bowers Harbor on Old Mission; Good Harbor, Blustone Vineyards, and Chateau de Leelanau on Leelanau; and Fenn Valley or Domaine Berrien Cellars (Pinot Gris) along Lake Michigan Shore.

Sauvignon Blanc

Michigan Sauvignon Blanc is less common on tasting menus than Riesling or Chardonnay, but when it appears it usually shows the cool-climate side of the grape: grapefruit, fresh herbs, and a zippy finish rather than tropical ripeness. Ask what is pouring that day — lists rotate, and staff can point you to the crispest whites on the board if a dedicated Sauvignon Blanc is not open.

Gewürztraminer and Similar Aromatics

Gewürztraminer brings lychee, rose petal, spice, and a richer texture — a favorite when someone wants a fragrant white that is not Riesling. Related cool-climate aromatics such as Pinot Blanc show up too, often as orchard-fruit whites with a softer edge.

Look for Gewürztraminer at Peninsula Cellars, Brys Estate, Ciccone Vineyard & Winery, Bel Lago, and Blustone. Pinot Blanc appears at estates such as Brys Estate, 45 North, and Left Foot Charley.

Michigan Red Wines

Michigan red wine is built for the table: brighter fruit, softer tannin, and more freshness than heavy, high-alcohol reds from warmer regions. Pinot Noir and Cabernet Franc lead the conversation; Cabernet Sauvignon and blends appear more often in Southwest Michigan and at estates that lean into Bordeaux-style plantings.

Pinot Noir

Michigan Pinot Noir often tastes of cherry, raspberry, and gentle spice, with a silky rather than chewy texture. It is a natural pair for salmon, mushroom dishes, and lighter meats — and a good answer when someone asks for a Michigan red that is not heavy.

Taste it at 2 Lads Winery, Chateau Chantal, and Bonobo on Old Mission; Bel Lago, Shady Lane Cellars, and Black Star Farms — Leelanau on the Leelanau trail.

Cabernet Franc

Michigan Cabernet Franc is one of the state’s strongest red stories: red berry fruit, a hint of herb or violet, and enough structure for food without the weight of warmer-climate Cabernet Sauvignon. Many tasting rooms pour it as a single variety and in blends.

On Old Mission, 2 Lads and Mari Vineyards are known for Cabernet Franc. On Leelanau, try Shady Lane or Gill’s Pier. Along Lake Michigan Shore, Domaine Berrien Cellars lists Cabernet Franc alongside Lemberger and Traminette.

Cabernet Sauvignon and Blends

Michigan Cabernet Sauvignon is less widespread than Cabernet Franc, but Southwest Michigan and a handful of other estates grow and bottle it. Expect darker fruit than Pinot, with a cooler, leaner profile than Napa-style Cabernet. Blends — Meritage-style mixes, signature reds, or house cuvées — are common when vintners want a fuller Michigan red wine for the table.

Examples from our listings include Free Run Cellars on Lake Michigan Shore (Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Syrah), Fenn Valley (Meritage), and Brys Estate (Signature Red Blend). Bos Farmhouse on Leelanau also lists Cabernet Sauvignon among its known wines.

Michigan Sparkling Wine

Cool nights and bright acidity make Michigan a natural home for bubbles. Styles range from traditional-method bottles to fresher, patio-friendly sparkling Riesling and Capriccio-style pours.

For a dedicated sparkling house on Leelanau, visit L. Mawby, known for Méthode Champenoise and Blanc de Blancs. Nearby, Hawthorne Vineyards and Shady Lane Cellars (Sparkling Riesling) expand the bubble options. On Old Mission, Chateau Chantal lists sparkling wine among its known pours. In Fennville, Fenn Valley is known for Capriccio sparkling — a useful Southwest stop if your trip is based near Saugatuck or Holland.

Fruit Wines, Cherry, Currant & Cider

Michigan’s orchards and berry farms share the same lakeshore climate as the vineyards, so fruit wines and hard cider are part of the tasting-day story — not a side note. Visitors searching for Michigan fruit wines, cherry wine, or Michigan currant wine will find dedicated farm wineries as well as grape estates that pour a fruit option alongside Riesling and reds.

  • Cherry wine — tart cherry, sometimes sweetened for dessert pours; common on both peninsulas. Try Peninsula Cellars, Good Harbor, or 45 North.
  • Currant and berry wines — black currant, gooseberry, blueberry, and mixed berry wines from farm wineries. Currant Mist Winery is known for Currant Wine and Gooseberry Wine; Coon’s Berry Farm & Winery and Henry’s Heritage Blueberry Winery focus on berry fruit wines.
  • Broader fruit wine programs — Lemon Creek Winery and Lazy Ballerina Winery on Lake Michigan Shore, Chateau de Leelanau on Leelanau, and Meckley’s Flavor Fruit Farm in Central Michigan.
  • Hard cider — orchard-driven and often bone-dry or farmhouse in style. Virtue Cider in Fennville and urban-leaning Left Foot Charley in Traverse City are natural stops if someone in your group wants a break from grape wine.

Where to Taste by Region

Match the style you want to the trail you are driving. You do not need to chase every grape in one day — pick a cluster and go deep.

  1. Old Mission Peninsula — Riesling, Chardonnay, Pinot Grigio, Cabernet Franc, and Pinot Noir in a compact loop near Traverse City. Compare it with Leelanau in our peninsula guide.
  2. Leelanau Peninsula — Riesling, sparkling specialists, Pinot Noir, Gewürztraminer, and cherry or fruit pours mixed into many tasting menus.
  3. Lake Michigan Shore / Southwest — A wider red and hybrid range, Capriccio-style sparkling, fruit wines, and cider around Fennville and Berrien County.
  4. Statewide browsing — Use the directory or wineries map guide when you want to filter by region before you leave home.

Based in Traverse City and want a designated driver? Book a guided day with Traverse City Wine Tours — hotel pickup and a planned Old Mission or Leelanau route so you can taste freely. More self-guided ideas live on our wine tours page, and lodging notes are on Plan Your Visit.

Ready to Pour?

You do not need to memorize every grape before your first stop. Lead with Michigan Riesling or a crisp white, add a Pinot Noir or Cabernet Franc, and leave room for sparkling or a fruit wine if the tasting room pours them.
